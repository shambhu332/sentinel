## Abstract

This thesis presents SENTINEL, an intelligent multi-agent automated Android application security assessment framework that integrates Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) knowledge retrieval with Large Language Model (LLM) triage to address critical deficiencies in contemporary mobile security assessment practice. Developed in response to the acute shortage of qualified mobile security professionals in Nepal's Kathmandu Valley, where fewer than twelve certified practitioners serve a rapidly expanding fintech ecosystem processing over NPR 200 billion monthly, SENTINEL orchestrates multi-specialised agents across a ten-phase assessment pipeline covering all fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories. The framework employs ChromaDB as its vector store with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embeddings (384 dimensions, cosine similarity) to retrieve the four most contextually relevant passages from a corpus of 2,323 knowledge items drawn from OWASP MASVS v2.1, CWE, and OSV. LLM triage is performed by Groq Llama 3.3 70B at temperature 0.0 for deterministic output, with Cerebras and Ollama as fallback providers. A seven-level proof gate reduces 8,420 raw detection flags to 534 confirmed vulnerabilities.

Evaluated against a 47-APK corpus including Nepalese fintech applications, SENTINEL achieved 94.3% vulnerability detection coverage, an 8.3% false positive rate, and an average assessment time of 8.3 minutes. These results represent statistically significant improvements over MobSF (52.7% coverage, 38.1% FPR), QARK (41.2% coverage, 42.4% FPR), and manual expert testing (53.4% coverage, 31.2% FPR, 7.4 hours). In answer to Research Question 1, SENTINEL confirms that multi-specialised agent orchestration with RAG and LLM triage substantially improves detection outcomes. In answer to Research Question 2, the embedded seven-level proof gate with mandatory Level 6 human approval provides a governance model sufficient for GDPR compliance and alignment with Nepal Rastra Bank Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023). The framework is released under MIT licence to support the Nepalese cybersecurity community.

## 01 · Introduction

The global cybersecurity landscape is undergoing a period of unprecedented transformation. As organisations increasingly rely on mobile applications to deliver financial services, healthcare, government functions, and commerce, the attack surface exposed through Android applications has expanded at a rate that human practitioners alone cannot adequately monitor. Global cybersecurity spending is projected to reach USD 345 billion by 2026 (Gartner, 2024), a figure that reflects both the scale of the threat environment and the economic burden placed on organisations attempting to maintain defensible postures. In the Kathmandu Valley of Nepal, this transformation is felt with particular acuity: a vibrant fintech ecosystem has emerged over the past decade, driven by smartphone adoption rates approaching 72% of the adult population and a regulatory mandate from Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) requiring periodic security assessments of all licenced financial applications. Yet the professional infrastructure required to conduct those assessments — qualified mobile security practitioners, sophisticated toolchains, and validated assessment methodologies — remains critically underdeveloped relative to the pace of technological growth.

[Figure 2 — Keywords Word Cloud for the SENTINEL Research Domain]

Android commands approximately 83% of the global smartphone operating system market (Statista, 2024), making it the dominant platform on which mobile applications are delivered worldwide and, consequently, the primary target of mobile-focused threat actors. The architecture of Android, with its rich inter-process communication mechanisms, complex permission model, native code layer, and diverse deployment environment, creates a multidimensional vulnerability surface that existing automated assessment tools address only partially. Tools such as MobSF and QARK have made meaningful contributions to the field, but neither provides systematic coverage of all fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories, nor does either embed the contextual knowledge enrichment or human oversight governance required to translate raw detection flags into auditable, actionable security verdicts.

The research gap that this thesis addresses can be stated concisely: no existing open-source or commercially available Android security assessment framework simultaneously achieves high detection coverage across the complete OWASP MASVS category taxonomy, maintains false positive rates acceptable for practitioner use, embeds a structured ethical governance model, and operates at an assessment throughput compatible with the resource constraints of Nepalese fintech organisations.

This thesis proposes the design and development of SENTINEL — Security ENforcement Through INtelligent EvaLuation — an intelligent multi-agent automated Android application security assessment framework. SENTINEL comprises five principal technical components: (1) a multi-specialised agent architecture that deploys category-specific assessment agents across all fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability domains; (2) a Retrieval-Augmented Generation knowledge pipeline that enriches each agent's assessment with contextually retrieved passages from a 2,323-item corpus spanning OWASP MASVS v2.1, CWE, and OSV advisories; (3) a Large Language Model triage router that invokes Groq Llama 3.3 70B at temperature 0.0 to produce deterministic, structured vulnerability verdicts; (4) a seven-level proof gate that filters raw detection signals through progressive evidence corroboration, Frida dynamic instrumentation, and mandatory human approval before any vulnerability is classified as confirmed; and (5) a three-tier memory architecture combining Redis hot cache, PostgreSQL relational storage, and MinIO cold object storage to maintain assessment state, audit trails, and forensic artefacts in compliance with NRB data handling requirements.

The evaluation corpus for this research comprises 47 Android APKs including publicly distributed Nepalese fintech applications, selected to ensure direct relevance to the regulatory environment in which SENTINEL is intended to operate. The experimental results, which are presented in detail in Section 18, demonstrate that SENTINEL achieves 94.3% vulnerability detection coverage, an 8.3% false positive rate, and a mean assessment time of 8.3 minutes — outcomes that represent substantial improvements over each of the comparative baseline tools evaluated. The thesis is structured to move from theoretical foundations through design decisions, ethical governance, and empirical validation, providing a complete account of how SENTINEL was conceived, built, and assessed.

## 02 · Problem Context and Motivation

Most organisational Android application security assessment practices are not grounded in a systematic, evidence-based framework that covers the complete vulnerability taxonomy defined by authoritative standards bodies, integrates contextual knowledge enrichment from curated security intelligence, or embeds the governance mechanisms necessary to ensure that assessment outcomes are both technically reliable and ethically defensible. This deficit is acute in Nepal, where the rapid digitalisation of financial services has created a threat environment that outpaces the professional capacity available to address it.

Nepal's fintech sector has grown remarkably over the past five years, with mobile payment platforms, digital wallets, and banking applications collectively processing over NPR 200 billion in monthly transactions as of 2023 (NRB, 2023). Applications such as eSewa, Khalti, ConnectIPS, and the mobile banking offerings of major commercial banks have become critical financial infrastructure for millions of Nepali citizens, many of whom have no alternative access to formal financial services. The security of these applications is therefore not merely a technical matter but a question of financial inclusion, economic stability, and national resilience. Yet Nepal Rastra Bank's own assessment data indicates that fewer than twelve certified mobile security testers operate within the country (NRB, 2023), and that 58% of Nepalese fintech applications assessed during regulatory review cycles contain vulnerabilities rated HIGH or CRITICAL according to the Common Vulnerability Scoring System. Fewer than 20% of the security assessments mandated by NRB IT Guidelines (NRB, 2012) are conducted at the depth of analysis those guidelines specify.

[Figure 3 — Nepal Mobile Cybersecurity Threat Landscape]

The causes of this situation are multiple and interlocking, and cannot be reduced to a single failure of technology or policy. Four root causes have been identified through analysis of the existing literature and direct engagement with the Nepalese security context. The first is workforce scarcity: the global shortage of 3.5 million unfilled cybersecurity positions (ISC², 2023) is felt with disproportionate severity in developing economies such as Nepal, where incentive structures, training infrastructure, and certification pathways have not developed at the pace of demand. The second is tool fragmentation: existing open-source assessment tools address different subsets of the vulnerability taxonomy and produce outputs in incompatible formats, requiring practitioners to integrate multiple toolchains manually — a process that introduces inconsistency and demands technical expertise that is itself in short supply. The third is cognitive overload: the volume of alerts generated by pattern-matching static analysis tools is sufficient to produce the alert fatigue documented by Sarker et al. (2020), in which practitioners become desensitised to security warnings and fail to distinguish genuine high-severity findings from false positives. The fourth is governance deficit: few organisations in Nepal's fintech sector have implemented the authorisation management, audit trail maintenance, and human oversight mechanisms that responsible automated security assessment requires.

[Figure 4 — Four Root Causes of Android Security Assessment Failures]

These four root causes interact with cognitive factors identified in behavioural economics research. Kahneman (2011) and Kahneman and Tversky (1979) demonstrate that human decision-making under uncertainty is systematically distorted by cognitive biases that cause individuals to deviate from normatively rational choices. In the security assessment context, loss aversion causes practitioners to weight the cost of acting on a false positive — investigating a non-existent vulnerability — more heavily than the benefit of catching a true positive, leading to under-investigation of ambiguous alerts. The availability heuristic causes practitioners to assess risk based on the ease with which examples come to mind, meaning that recently publicised vulnerability classes receive disproportionate attention while less-salient but equally serious categories are neglected. Confirmation bias causes analysts who form an initial hypothesis about an application's security posture to seek evidence that confirms that hypothesis rather than evidence that would disconfirm it. SENTINEL addresses each of these cognitive biases through design choices that are detailed in subsequent sections: the seven-level proof gate addresses loss aversion by providing graduated evidence before requiring analyst commitment; multi-specialised agent coverage across all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories addresses the availability heuristic by ensuring systematic rather than salience-driven assessment; and independent multi-agent corroboration at Level 2 of the proof gate addresses confirmation bias by requiring evidence from agents operating on different analysis vectors before a finding is escalated.

The interdisciplinary nature of the problem is itself significant. SENTINEL draws simultaneously on computer science (distributed systems, machine learning, natural language processing), behavioural psychology (cognitive bias theory, human-automation interaction), and applied cybersecurity (vulnerability taxonomy, penetration testing methodology, regulatory compliance). This interdisciplinary framing is not incidental but essential: a purely technical solution to the Android security assessment problem would be incomplete, because the technical components of SENTINEL are designed in explicit response to the behavioural and governance failures that prevent existing tools from being used effectively.

## 03 · Cybersecurity Theories and Behavioural Factors in Security Decision-Making

A rigorous theoretical foundation is prerequisite to the design of any security assessment framework that aspires to measurable improvement over its predecessors. SENTINEL is grounded in two complementary theoretical traditions: the OWASP MASVS taxonomy as the normative framework for systematic vulnerability assessment, and Prospect Theory as articulated by Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and extended by Kahneman (2011) as the explanatory framework for understanding why human practitioners fail to achieve the systematic coverage that OWASP MASVS specifies. Together, these theoretical streams motivate specific design choices in SENTINEL's architecture, ensuring that theoretical insight is translated into measurable engineering outcomes rather than remaining at the level of abstract justification.

The OWASP Mobile Application Security Verification Standard (MASVS) version 2.1 provides the most comprehensive and widely adopted taxonomy of Android application security requirements currently available (OWASP, 2023). It organises 273 security controls into fourteen vulnerability categories spanning storage security, cryptography, authentication, network communication, platform interaction, code quality, resilience against reverse engineering, and several others. By structuring SENTINEL's multi-specialised agent deployment around these fourteen categories — assigning a dedicated agent cluster with category-specific pattern libraries and payload sets to each — the framework ensures that no category of vulnerability is systematically neglected due to practitioner preference, time pressure, or cognitive salience effects. This is not merely an organisational convenience but a direct theoretical commitment: systematic category coverage is the engineering realisation of the normative requirement that security assessment be comprehensive rather than selective.

Prospect Theory, introduced by Kahneman and Tversky (1979) as an empirically grounded alternative to Expected Utility Theory, demonstrates that individuals evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, exhibit loss aversion (losses loom approximately 2.5 times larger than equivalent gains in psychological impact), overweight low-probability events, and are subject to framing effects that cause identical information to be processed differently depending on how it is presented. Kahneman (2011) extends this analysis to the distinction between System 1 (fast, associative, heuristic) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical) thinking, demonstrating that human practitioners under time pressure or cognitive load default to System 1 processing, which is particularly vulnerable to the availability heuristic and confirmation bias.

In the context of Android security assessment, loss aversion manifests as reluctance to escalate ambiguous findings to confirmed vulnerabilities, because the perceived cost of being wrong — wasted investigation effort, damaged credibility with developers — outweighs the perceived benefit of catching a true positive. SENTINEL's seven-level proof gate addresses this directly by structuring the evidence presentation at Level 6 (mandatory human approval) so that the analyst receives the full evidence chain — static pattern detection, multi-agent corroboration, LLM verdict with confidence score, and Frida dynamic confirmation — before being asked to make a classification decision. This evidence pre-assembly shifts the analyst's reference point: rather than evaluating a raw alert against the cost of investigation, the analyst evaluates a comprehensively evidenced finding against the cost of endorsement, substantially reducing the loss aversion distortion.

The availability heuristic, whereby practitioners over-assess risk in vulnerability categories that have recently featured in publicised breaches and under-assess risk in categories that have received less media attention, is addressed by SENTINEL's mandatory multi-specialised agent dispatch across all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories for every assessment. Because no category can be omitted by practitioner preference, the assessment outcome is independent of which vulnerability classes currently dominate security news cycles in Nepal or globally. Confirmation bias, whereby an analyst who forms an early hypothesis about an application's security posture selectively seeks confirming evidence, is addressed at Level 2 of the proof gate, which requires corroboration from at least two independent agents operating on different analysis vectors before a finding can progress. This structural independence between evidence sources prevents the cascade of confirmatory reasoning that confirmation bias would otherwise produce.

[Figure 5 — Prospect Theory Mapped onto the Seven-Level Proof Gate]

[Figure 6 — Seven-Level Proof Gate Detail]

The theoretical synthesis that SENTINEL embodies can be stated precisely: OWASP MASVS provides the normative standard of what a comprehensive assessment must cover; Prospect Theory explains why human practitioners systematically fail to achieve that standard when operating without structured support; and SENTINEL's architecture provides the engineering mechanisms that compensate for the identified cognitive failures while preserving the irreplaceable human judgement that remains necessary for responsible security assessment. This is the "complement not replace" framing that distinguishes SENTINEL from fully automated assessment approaches: the framework is designed to extend human analytical capacity rather than to substitute for human authority, a distinction that is operationally enforced by the mandatory Level 6 human approval gate that prevents any vulnerability from being classified as confirmed without analyst endorsement.

The application of Prospect Theory to security tool design is not without precedent, but its systematic translation into specific architectural features of a multi-agent assessment framework represents a novel contribution that this thesis develops in detail. Cross-domain validation of behavioural economics theory applied to system design can be found in user interface research (where loss aversion framing has been used to motivate interface design choices), public health communication (where availability heuristic effects have been used to design information presentation strategies), and financial product design (where confirmation bias has motivated the design of investment decision support systems). SENTINEL applies the same theoretical apparatus to the design of a security assessment framework, with the specific adaptation that the "decisions" in question are security classification decisions made by analysts under conditions of informational uncertainty and time pressure — conditions that are precisely those under which Kahneman's research demonstrates cognitive biases are most potent.

## 04 · Optimising Security Assessment via RAG and Large Language Models

The integration of Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Large Language Models into security assessment workflows represents a convergence of research streams that, until recently, remained largely separate: the natural language processing literature on knowledge-grounded generation, the cybersecurity literature on automated vulnerability classification, and the human-computer interaction literature on human-AI collaboration in high-stakes decision environments. Understanding the contribution of SENTINEL's RAG-LLM pipeline requires situating it within this broader context of cross-domain validation, examining how similar architectures have performed in analogous domains, and identifying the specific adaptations required to make these architectures effective in the Android security assessment context.

RAG, as formalised by Lewis et al. (2020), addresses a fundamental limitation of large language models trained on static corpora: their inability to incorporate domain-specific, frequently updated, or proprietary knowledge that was not present in their training data, or that has changed since training. The RAG architecture resolves this by maintaining a separate knowledge corpus, embedding both corpus documents and input queries in a shared vector space, and retrieving the most semantically similar corpus passages at inference time to augment the model's prompt with relevant context. Gao et al. (2023) provide a comprehensive survey of RAG applications demonstrating strong performance in legal document analysis, medical diagnosis support, and financial instrument classification — all domains characterised by a structured, authoritative knowledge base, high-stakes decisions, and the need to ground outputs in citable evidence rather than model intuition. The structural parallels to Android security assessment are direct: OWASP MASVS and CWE constitute the authoritative knowledge base, vulnerability classification is the high-stakes decision, and NRB audit requirements demand that assessment verdicts be grounded in citable security standards.

Existing Android security tools that incorporate AI components do so in a fragmented manner that does not realise the full potential of the RAG-LLM integration. MobSF incorporates some machine learning components for malware classification but does not ground its analysis in a retrieved knowledge corpus or produce LLM-quality natural language explanations. QARK relies on pattern matching without any AI enrichment. Commercial tools such as Checkmarx and Veracode incorporate AI triage but do not expose the knowledge sources underlying their verdicts, making it impossible for practitioners or regulators to verify the evidential basis of findings — a critical deficiency in the NRB compliance context. SENTINEL addresses these gaps by making the RAG retrieval step fully transparent: every assessment report records the specific OWASP MASVS controls, CWE entries, and OSV advisories that were retrieved and used to contextualise the LLM prompt, enabling practitioners and auditors to trace the evidential basis of any verdict.

[Figure 7 — SENTINEL RAG Pipeline from Corpus to Contextual Knowledge]

SENTINEL's RAG pipeline is implemented using ChromaDB as the vector store, with sentence embeddings generated by the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model producing 384-dimensional vectors. Similarity retrieval uses cosine distance, and the top four most similar passages are retrieved for each query. The knowledge corpus comprises 2,323 passages drawn from three authoritative sources: OWASP MASVS v2.1 (273 controls), the Common Weakness Enumeration (1,200 entries), and the Open Source Vulnerabilities database (850 advisories). Retrieval latency is 12 to 18 milliseconds per query, measured in the evaluation environment, which is sufficiently fast to avoid introducing meaningful delay into the assessment pipeline. The choice of all-MiniLM-L6-v2 reflects a deliberate optimisation for the deployment context: unlike larger embedding models, it runs efficiently on CPU-only infrastructure, making it compatible with the hardware available to Nepalese fintech security teams who cannot be assumed to have access to GPU compute resources.

The LLM triage component employs a three-provider priority chain designed to maximise assessment reliability while accommodating the variable network conditions and service availability that characterise the Kathmandu Valley internet infrastructure. Groq Llama 3.3 70B serves as the primary provider, selected for its combination of strong vulnerability classification capability, fast inference through Groq's dedicated hardware, and the open-weight nature of the underlying Llama 3.3 architecture that permits academic scrutiny of model characteristics. Cerebras serves as the secondary provider, offering an alternative inference path with different hardware characteristics. Ollama running qwen2.5-coder:7b serves as the local fallback, ensuring that assessment can continue even when external API connectivity is unavailable — a design choice motivated by the intermittent connectivity issues documented in the Kathmandu Valley digital infrastructure. All three providers are configured with temperature 0.0, ensuring that LLM outputs are deterministic and reproducible: the same vulnerability evidence will always produce the same structured verdict, a property essential for audit trail integrity.

[Figure 8 — LLM Triage Router Three-Provider Priority Chain]

The integration of RAG context with LLM triage produces qualitatively different outputs than either component alone would generate. RAG without LLM produces retrieved passages that must be manually interpreted by analysts, providing knowledge context but not classification decisions. LLM without RAG produces classification decisions grounded in training data that may be outdated, incomplete, or insufficiently specific to the OWASP MASVS control framework. The combination produces structured, evidence-grounded verdict documents that specify the vulnerability category, CVSS v3.1 score, supporting OWASP MASVS controls, CWE identifiers, relevant OSV advisories, and a natural language explanation of the finding — all in a JSON format that is both machine-processable for downstream pipeline stages and human-readable for analyst review at the Level 6 approval gate. This combination is central to SENTINEL's claim to represent an advance over existing tools and is validated empirically by the evaluation results presented in Section 18.

## 05 · Research Aim

The primary aim of this research is to design, develop, and empirically validate SENTINEL: an intelligent multi-agent automated Android application security assessment framework that integrates Retrieval-Augmented Generation knowledge retrieval with Large Language Model triage to achieve demonstrably superior vulnerability detection coverage, false positive rates, and assessment throughput compared to existing single-method automated and manual security assessment approaches, while embedding a structured ethical governance model that ensures the framework operates responsibly and in compliance with GDPR principles and Nepal Rastra Bank regulatory requirements — thereby addressing the acute deficit in mobile application security assessment capacity documented in Nepal's Kathmandu Valley fintech ecosystem.

This primary aim is realised through five component objectives that together constitute a complete research programme:

- To analyse the theoretical and practical limitations of current Android security assessment practice, with particular reference to cognitive biases, tool fragmentation, and governance deficits in the Nepalese fintech context.
- To investigate the state of the art in Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Large Language Model triage, and multi-agent system architectures, identifying the design patterns most applicable to the Android vulnerability assessment domain.
- To conduct systematic case study analysis of existing tools — MobSF, QARK, manual expert testing, and commercial AI-assisted platforms — to identify specific capability gaps that SENTINEL must address.
- To design and develop SENTINEL as a functioning software framework, implementing the ten-phase assessment pipeline, multi-specialised agent architecture, RAG knowledge pipeline, seven-level proof gate, and three-tier memory system.
- To evaluate SENTINEL against a 47-APK corpus including Nepalese fintech applications, measure performance against three primary metrics (detection coverage, false positive rate, assessment time), and document the ethical governance audit results.

## 07 · Contribution and Significance

SENTINEL makes three primary and distinct contributions to knowledge that together justify its claim to represent a meaningful advance over the existing state of practice in Android application security assessment.

The technical contribution consists in the novel combination of three capabilities that have not previously been integrated within a single open-source Android assessment framework: multi-specialised agent orchestration across all fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories; RAG-enriched contextual knowledge retrieval from a curated 2,323-item security intelligence corpus; and a seven-level proof gate that progressively filters raw detection signals through corroboration, dynamic instrumentation, and mandatory human approval before any finding is confirmed. Individual elements of this combination — static analysis, dynamic instrumentation, LLM classification — are available in existing tools, but their integration within a single, coherent, auditable pipeline represents a genuine technical innovation. The proof gate architecture in particular, which reduces 8,420 raw detection flags to 534 confirmed vulnerabilities, provides a false positive suppression mechanism that no existing open-source Android security tool offers at equivalent scale. The scikit-learn GradientBoostingClassifier (n_estimators=50, max_depth=3) used to select optimal agent retry strategies further demonstrates the framework's sophisticated approach to computational resource allocation under uncertainty.

The academic contribution consists in the provision of an empirically validated comparative study of Android security assessment methodologies evaluated against a corpus that explicitly includes Nepalese fintech applications. Prior academic literature on Android security assessment has been conducted primarily in Western or East Asian contexts; this thesis provides, to the author's knowledge, the first comparative evaluation study that includes Nepalese fintech APKs and explicitly contextualises findings against NRB regulatory requirements. The evaluation methodology — 47 APKs, three primary metrics, four comparative baselines, Docker-isolated execution environment, and temporal validation for machine learning components — is documented in sufficient detail to support replication, providing a reference methodology for future Android security assessment research in developing economy contexts.

The ethical contribution consists in the design and validation of a seven-layer ethical governance model that is not a post-hoc policy document but an integral engineering component of the framework. The authorisation gate at Phase 1, the per-scan Docker isolation, the full LLM verdict transparency record, and the mandatory Level 6 human approval are not optional configurations but architectural constraints enforced by the framework's control flow. The demonstration that this governance model satisfies GDPR Article 5 data minimisation requirements and NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023) compliance criteria — validated through structured expert audit — provides a template that other developers of AI-assisted security tools in the Nepal context can adopt and adapt.

[Figure 11 — SENTINEL Three Primary Contributions to Knowledge]

The significance of these contributions is amplified by the deployment context. Nepal's fintech sector is growing rapidly, NRB's regulatory requirements are becoming more demanding, and the professional capacity to conduct compliant security assessments is acutely insufficient. SENTINEL, released under MIT licence, provides an immediately deployable tool that Nepalese fintech firms, their security assessors, and NRB itself can use to improve the depth and consistency of mobile application security assessment without requiring the engagement of expensive international consultants or proprietary commercial platforms. This democratisation of assessment capability is itself a contribution to the equitable development of cybersecurity capacity in an economy that has been underserved by both the commercial security industry and the academic research community.

## 08 · Justification of the Study

The justification for undertaking this research rests on a convergence of empirical evidence demonstrating that the current state of Android application security assessment in Nepal and globally is inadequate, that the technical and governance mechanisms required to address that inadequacy are available and have been validated in analogous domains, and that no existing framework integrates those mechanisms in a manner that meets the specific requirements of the Nepalese fintech context.

The global scale of the mobile security problem is well-documented. The Verizon Mobile Security Index (2024) reports that 69% of organisations surveyed experienced a compromise involving a mobile device in the preceding twelve months, and that mobile-specific threats accounted for a growing proportion of total security incidents. This figure reflects a global trend, but it is particularly significant in the context of Nepal, where mobile applications have become the primary interface between financial institutions and their customers, and where the consequences of a breach — for financial institutions, for customers, and for the integrity of the national payments system — are severe. Nepal Rastra Bank's own assessment data indicates that fewer than 20% of the security assessments mandated by NRB IT Guidelines (NRB, 2012) are conducted at the depth those guidelines specify (NRB, 2023), meaning that the regulatory safety net is itself insufficient.

[Figure 12 — Nepal Fintech Mobile Application Vulnerability Statistics]

The workforce dimension of the justification is equally compelling. ISC² (2023) estimates that there are 3.5 million unfilled cybersecurity positions globally, a figure that represents both the scale of unmet demand and the structural impossibility of addressing the mobile security assessment deficit through workforce expansion alone within any realistic planning horizon. Nepal, with fewer than twelve certified mobile security testers (NRB, 2023), exemplifies the extreme end of this global distribution. The implication is direct: automated frameworks that can extend the analytical capacity of a small number of skilled practitioners — performing the systematic, repeatable components of assessment and presenting enriched evidence for human judgement — are not merely desirable but necessary if the regulatory mandate is to be met.

The technical justification for RAG and LLM integration in security assessment draws on the foundational work of Sommer and Paxson (2010), who argue that the adversarial detection problem — distinguishing malicious from benign behaviour in a context where adversaries actively adapt to detection mechanisms — requires machine learning approaches that can generalise beyond the specific patterns observed in training data. RAG addresses this generalisation requirement by grounding detection not only in learned model weights but in a retrievable, updatable knowledge corpus that can incorporate newly published OWASP MASVS controls and CVE entries without model retraining. Lewis et al. (2020) and Gao et al. (2023) demonstrate that RAG architectures achieve strong performance in structured domain classification tasks precisely because the knowledge corpus provides the stable, authoritative reference that pure neural network approaches lack. The application of this insight to Android vulnerability classification — where OWASP MASVS v2.1 constitutes the authoritative reference — is theoretically well-motivated and empirically validated by the results of this study.

The NRB mandate context provides additional and distinctive justification that is specific to this research. Nepal Rastra Bank IT Guidelines (2012) require all licenced financial institutions to conduct periodic security assessments of their mobile applications and to maintain documented evidence of assessment findings for regulatory inspection. The NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023) elaborate these requirements, specifying that assessments must cover all OWASP MASVS categories and that evidence packages must include sufficient technical detail to support NRB examination. SENTINEL's ten-phase pipeline, three-tier memory architecture, and structured reporting module are designed specifically to produce the evidence packages that NRB requires — a design choice that has no parallel in any existing open-source Android assessment framework known to the author.

A realistic statement about the scope of SENTINEL's contribution is warranted. SENTINEL is not a complete substitute for human expert security assessment, and it does not claim to detect every possible Android vulnerability. Its evaluation corpus of 47 APKs, while representative of the Nepalese fintech context, is not sufficient to establish universal performance claims across all Android application categories. The controlled evaluation environment — Docker-isolated, with stable network conditions — does not replicate the complexity of production assessment environments. These limitations are documented honestly and are addressed in the future works programme described in Section 19. Within its defined scope and evaluation conditions, however, the evidence for SENTINEL's superiority over existing baseline tools is robust and consistent across all three primary metrics.

## 09 · Research Questions

Two research questions structure the empirical inquiry of this thesis, one technical and one ethical. The decision to formulate exactly two questions, rather than a single integrated question or a broader set of sub-questions, reflects a deliberate methodological choice: the technical and ethical dimensions of AI-assisted security assessment are sufficiently distinct in their evidence requirements, evaluation methods, and theoretical grounding to warrant separate formulation, yet sufficiently interconnected — through the proof gate's dual function as both a technical filter and an ethical governance mechanism — to remain parts of a unified research programme. The two-question structure is consistent with the approach adopted in comparable AI-assisted security research (Kramer et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2013) and with the dual-validation methodology described in Section 11.

**Research Question 1 (Technical):** "How can a multi-specialised agent framework integrating Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) knowledge retrieval and Large Language Model (LLM) triage improve Android APK vulnerability detection coverage and false positive rates compared to existing single-method automated assessment tools, when evaluated across a corpus of real-world applications including Nepalese fintech APKs?"

RQ1 is answered primarily through the technical evaluation reported in Section 18, which provides comparative metrics across SENTINEL and the four baseline approaches. The question is phrased to focus on mechanism ("how can") rather than mere outcome ("does"), reflecting the research's commitment to explaining why SENTINEL achieves the outcomes it does — through the RAG knowledge enrichment, multi-specialised agent category coverage, and proof gate false positive suppression — rather than simply asserting that it does so. The inclusion of Nepalese fintech APKs in the evaluation corpus is explicit in the question formulation because the relevance of the research to the Nepal context is a foundational design criterion, not an incidental feature of the evaluation.

**Research Question 2 (Ethical):** "What are the ethical implications of deploying an automated AI-driven Android application security assessment framework, and how can a structured governance model incorporating authorisation gates, privacy-preserving analysis, algorithmic transparency, and mandatory human oversight ensure responsible and auditable operation in line with GDPR principles and Nepal Rastra Bank regulatory requirements?"

RQ2 is answered through the ethical governance audit reported in Sections 17 and 18, which documents the performance of each governance mechanism against the criteria specified in GDPR Article 5 and NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023). The question's compound structure — "what are the implications" and "how can a governance model ensure" — reflects the two-stage ethical analysis: first identifying the ethical risks introduced by AI-assisted security assessment, then demonstrating that SENTINEL's governance architecture provides sufficient mitigation.

## 10 · Research Hypotheses

Two research hypotheses are formulated in correspondence with the two research questions. H1 is technical in character, specifying measurable outcomes that constitute confirmation or disconfirmation of the technical claim; H2 is ethical in character, employing the conditional structure appropriate to a claim about the relationship between governance mechanisms and risk mitigation.

**H1 (Technical Hypothesis):** "If a multi-specialised agent framework combining static decompilation analysis, dynamic Frida runtime instrumentation, RAG-enriched contextual knowledge retrieval, and LLM-assisted verdict classification is applied to Android APK security assessment across a representative corpus of applications, then vulnerability detection coverage will exceed eighty percent and false positive rate will fall below fifteen percent — representing statistically significant improvements over established single-method baseline tools MobSF, QARK, and manual analysis."

H1 specifies concrete quantitative thresholds — 80% detection coverage and 15% false positive rate — that distinguish confirmation from disconfirmation. These thresholds were selected based on analysis of the baseline tool performance documented in the comparative case studies (Section 14): MobSF's 38.1% false positive rate and 52.7% coverage represent the current state of open-source practice, and the 15% FPR and 80% coverage thresholds represent a substantial improvement that would be meaningful for practitioner use. H1 is evaluated in Section 18, where SENTINEL's results of 94.3% coverage and 8.3% FPR confirm the hypothesis with a substantial margin above the specified thresholds.

**H2 (Ethical Hypothesis):** "While multi-specialised agent automation can substantially enhance Android APK vulnerability detection coverage, reduce analyst workload, and produce assessment outputs at a fraction of the time required by manual methods, deployment without a structured ethical governance model incorporating authorisation verification, privacy-preserving analysis, algorithmic transparency mechanisms, mandatory human approval at the evidence confirmation stage, and harm-avoidance gating of exploit generation will introduce significant risks including unauthorised testing, data exposure, opaque AI verdicts, and inappropriate automated offensive action — risks that a seven-level proof gate with mandatory Level 6 human approval can substantially mitigate."

H2 employs the conditional structure "While X can do Y, without Z, W occurs" to acknowledge the genuine technical benefits of automation while insisting that those benefits are realised safely only when governance mechanisms are present. This structure prevents a false dichotomy between pro-automation and anti-automation positions: SENTINEL does not argue that automation is inherently dangerous, but rather that automation without governance is dangerous, and that the specific governance architecture instantiated in SENTINEL's proof gate provides the required mitigation. H2 is evaluated in Sections 17 and 18, where the ethical governance audit confirms that SENTINEL's governance mechanisms perform as specified.

H1 is confirmed by the empirical evaluation reported in Section 18, where all three quantitative thresholds are exceeded and the improvements over all three baseline comparators are consistent and substantial. H2 is confirmed by the ethical governance audit reported in Sections 17 and 18, where the authorisation gate's 100% rejection of unauthorised submissions, the privacy-preserving analysis mechanisms, the LLM verdict transparency record, and the 82.4% analyst endorsement rate at Level 6 together demonstrate that the governance model functions as designed. The conditional structure of H2 is particularly significant: it is not confirmed by showing that SENTINEL is ethical, but by showing that SENTINEL without its governance components would be dangerous, and that the governance components successfully mitigate the identified risks.

## 11 · Research Methodology

SENTINEL's research methodology integrates Design Science Research (Hevner et al., 2004) as the overarching epistemological framework with an empirical comparative evaluation design that produces the quantitative evidence required to answer both research questions. Design Science Research is the appropriate methodology for research that aims to produce a novel artefact — in this case, SENTINEL itself — and to evaluate that artefact against explicit performance criteria derived from the problem context. Hevner et al. (2004) specify that Design Science Research generates knowledge through the process of building and evaluating innovative artefacts, and that the validity of the knowledge produced depends on the rigour of both the design process and the evaluation methodology. SENTINEL satisfies both requirements: the design process is documented in sufficient detail to support replication (Section 16), and the evaluation methodology is structured around pre-specified metrics, comparative baselines, and controlled execution conditions.

The development methodology within the Design Science Research frame is Agile, structured as three six-week sprints over a six-month period. Sprint 1 addressed requirements analysis, theoretical foundation, and initial architecture design, producing the RAG corpus construction, ChromaDB indexing, and LLM router configuration. Sprint 2 addressed multi-specialised agent implementation, proof gate engineering, and three-tier memory integration, producing a functional assessment pipeline capable of end-to-end APK analysis. Sprint 3 addressed evaluation corpus selection, comparative baseline testing, ethical governance audit, and documentation, producing the empirical results reported in Section 18 and the thesis document itself. The Agile methodology was chosen over a waterfall approach because the iterative sprint structure permitted continuous refinement of design decisions in response to observed pipeline behaviour — a flexibility that proved particularly valuable when addressing the technical challenges of JSON output format variation and ADB race conditions documented in Section 16.

[Figure 15 — Agile Research Methodology Sprint Cycle]

The research employs methodological triangulation across four evidence sources: systematic literature analysis (addressed in Sections 03, 04, and 13), prototype development and technical testing (addressed in Section 16), comparative empirical evaluation (addressed in Section 18), and ethical governance audit (addressed in Sections 12 and 17). Triangulation is particularly important in Design Science Research because the artefact being evaluated — SENTINEL — is also the primary instrument of data collection, creating a potential circularity that is mitigated by the use of independent baseline comparators and structured audit criteria derived from external standards (GDPR, NRB Guidelines).

The evaluation corpus comprises 47 Android APKs selected to span a range of application categories relevant to the Nepalese fintech context: mobile banking applications, digital wallet platforms, payment gateways, and general-purpose applications for calibration purposes. Nepalese fintech applications were included explicitly to ensure that evaluation results are directly interpretable in the target deployment context. All APKs in the corpus are publicly distributed applications that do not require special authorisation for security research purposes; no proprietary or private applications were included without explicit written consent from the application developer, consistent with the authorisation requirements enforced by SENTINEL's own Phase 1 gate. All assessment execution was conducted within Docker-isolated containers to prevent data leakage between assessments and to ensure that evaluation conditions were reproducible.

Three primary evaluation metrics were pre-specified before evaluation commenced, following the principle of confirmatory rather than exploratory analysis. Detection coverage measures the proportion of known vulnerabilities in each APK (established through ground truth annotation by expert analysts) that SENTINEL identifies as detected at or above Level 3 of the proof gate. False positive rate measures the proportion of SENTINEL's confirmed findings (at Level 6) that do not correspond to genuine vulnerabilities in the ground truth annotation. Assessment time measures the elapsed clock time from APK submission to final report generation. These three metrics correspond directly to the three primary deficiencies of existing tools identified in the case study analysis: insufficient coverage (MobSF, QARK), excessive false positives (all three automated baselines), and excessive time cost (manual testing).

[Figure 16 — Comparative Evaluation Design]

The machine learning components of SENTINEL — specifically the scikit-learn GradientBoostingClassifier used for agent retry strategy selection — were subjected to temporal validation to guard against data leakage and overfitting. The training set for the classifier comprised APK assessments from the first two development sprints; the evaluation corpus for Section 18 was held out entirely from classifier training. This temporal separation ensures that the classifier's performance during evaluation reflects genuine generalisation rather than memorisation of evaluation cases.

The ethics dimension of the methodology is not confined to a discrete ethics review phase but is integrated throughout all five research phases. Requirements analysis incorporated ethical risk assessment; design incorporated governance mechanism specification; implementation incorporated authorisation gate and privacy controls; evaluation incorporated governance audit; and documentation incorporates honest acknowledgement of limitations and failure modes. This integration reflects the thesis's position that ethics is a design criterion rather than a compliance exercise.

## 12 · Ethical Considerations

The deployment of an automated AI-driven security assessment framework raises ethical questions that are both practically urgent and theoretically significant. SENTINEL's ethical governance architecture is structured around four ethical layers that together address the principal risks identified in the literature on AI-assisted security tools and human-AI collaboration in high-stakes decision environments. These four layers — authorisation, privacy, algorithmic transparency, and the boundary between automation and human authority — are implemented not as policy guidelines but as engineering constraints enforced by the framework's architecture, ensuring that ethical compliance is structural rather than discretionary.

The authorisation layer is implemented as a hard constraint at Phase 1 of the assessment pipeline. Every APK submitted to SENTINEL must be accompanied by a signed authorisation form naming the submitting organisation, the application being assessed, the scope and purpose of the assessment, and the date on which authorisation was granted by the application owner or their designated representative. SENTINEL validates this form before any analysis commences; submissions without a valid authorisation form are rejected without exception. This is not an advisory recommendation but an architectural constraint: the pipeline control flow cannot proceed past Phase 1 without a positive authorisation validation, and there is no administrative override. During the evaluation period, this gate rejected 100% of submissions that lacked authorisation documentation, including test submissions made without authorisation to verify gate function. The rationale for making authorisation a hard constraint rather than a soft warning reflects the legal and ethical severity of unauthorised security testing, which constitutes a criminal offence under the Computer Misuse Act and equivalent Nepalese legislation, and the recognition that a framework that permits unauthorised testing — even inadvertently — creates legal liability for both the framework developer and the organisation deploying it.

The privacy layer addresses the risk that security assessment produces data about application users and system administrators that was not obtained with their consent and should not be retained beyond the minimum necessary period. SENTINEL implements privacy protection through three mechanisms: per-scan Docker container isolation, which prevents any data from one assessment leaking into another assessment's environment; automated PII marker detection and flagging, which identifies strings matching patterns associated with personally identifiable information (names, identity card numbers, telephone numbers, financial account identifiers) and flags them for analyst review rather than processing them directly; and automated deletion of transient assessment data upon scan completion, retaining only the structured finding records and evidence packages required for NRB audit compliance. These mechanisms implement GDPR Article 5(1)(c) data minimisation requirements, ensuring that SENTINEL processes only the personal data necessary for its specified assessment purpose and retains that data only for as long as regulatory requirements mandate.

The algorithmic transparency layer addresses the risk that AI-assisted verdicts are opaque — that practitioners and regulators cannot understand or verify the reasoning by which an LLM arrived at a vulnerability classification. Every verdict record produced by SENTINEL includes: the model identifier and version of the LLM used for triage; the temperature setting applied (0.0 in all production assessments); the SHA-256 hash of the prompt template used to structure the LLM query; the identifiers of the four RAG passages retrieved from ChromaDB and used to contextualise the prompt; and the full structured JSON verdict document produced by the LLM. This transparency record is stored in PostgreSQL as part of the permanent audit trail and included in the NRB evidence package. Three cybersecurity professionals with NRB audit experience reviewed sample transparency records during the evaluation period and confirmed that the level of detail provided was sufficient to support regulatory examination of assessment methodology.

The human oversight layer is implemented through the mandatory Level 6 approval gate, which requires explicit analyst endorsement before any vulnerability can be classified as CONFIRMED and before the Level 7 PoC generation capability can be activated. The word "mandatory" is used with precision: there is no pathway by which SENTINEL classifies a vulnerability as CONFIRMED without a human analyst's explicit endorsement. This constraint is implemented in the framework's database schema — the CONFIRMED status cannot be written to the findings table without a corresponding analyst endorsement record — making it architecturally impossible to bypass. Liao et al. (2013) demonstrate that human-AI collaboration outperforms fully automated systems in high-stakes classification contexts precisely because the combination of machine systematic coverage and human contextual judgement produces superior outcomes to either alone. SENTINEL instantiates this principle by ensuring that the analyst at Level 6 receives the full evidence chain — static detection, multi-agent corroboration, LLM verdict with confidence score, and Frida dynamic confirmation — and is required to engage with that evidence rather than simply approve a machine recommendation.

[Figure 17 — Ethical Governance Framework Seven Principles]

The harm avoidance dimension of the ethical architecture addresses the risk that security assessment tooling generates proof-of-concept exploit code that is misused. SENTINEL's Level 7 PoC generation module produces read-only documentation of exploitation techniques and is activated only after Level 6 analyst approval, only for findings classified as CONFIRMED, and only when the submitting organisation's authorisation form explicitly includes PoC documentation in the assessment scope. The PoC output does not include executable exploit code; it describes the exploitation pathway in sufficient detail for remediation planning but does not provide a turnkey offensive capability.

The risk of analyst skill atrophy — the concern that practitioners who rely on AI-assisted assessment become less capable of performing independent assessment — is addressed through the evidence chain presentation design at Level 6. Rather than presenting the analyst with a simple approve/reject decision, the Level 6 interface requires the analyst to review the full evidence chain and document the basis for their endorsement or rejection decision. This design maintains analyst engagement with the underlying vulnerability evidence and prevents the passive rubber-stamping of AI verdicts that would produce skill atrophy over time.

## 13 · Literature Review

### Overview

The literature relevant to SENTINEL's design and evaluation spans four distinct strands: the evolution of Android security assessment methodologies from signature-based pattern matching through statistical anomaly detection to the current generation of AI-assisted tools; the behavioural economics literature on cognitive bias in security decision-making; the natural language processing literature on Retrieval-Augmented Generation and LLM-assisted classification; and the human-computer interaction literature on human-AI collaboration in high-stakes domains. Each strand contributes a different dimension to the theoretical and empirical foundation of this research, and each is represented in the design choices that SENTINEL embodies. This section reviews each strand in turn before synthesising the gaps that SENTINEL is designed to address. The Nepal fintech context runs as a connecting thread throughout, grounding the literature review in the specific deployment environment that motivates this research.

### Traditional and AI-Assisted Android Security Systems

The history of Android application security assessment tools can be understood as a sequence of methodological generations, each addressing limitations identified in its predecessors while introducing new constraints that motivate the next generation. The first generation relied on signature-based pattern matching: tools maintained databases of known-malicious code patterns and flagged applications that contained matching sequences. This approach achieved acceptable detection rates for known vulnerability patterns but was entirely ineffective against novel attack techniques, obfuscated code, and semantic vulnerabilities that depend on the relationship between code elements rather than the content of individual patterns. The second generation applied statistical anomaly detection, learning distributions of application behaviour from large corpora and flagging applications whose behaviour fell outside learned norms. Sommer and Paxson (2010) provide the canonical analysis of the limitations of this approach in the adversarial detection context, demonstrating that the high-dimensional nature of application behaviour space and the active adaptation of adversarial actors to detection mechanisms creates fundamental challenges for statistical detection that cannot be addressed by improving the statistical models alone.

The third generation, represented by tools such as MobSF and QARK, combines static decompilation analysis with rule-based vulnerability detection, achieving broader coverage of the vulnerability taxonomy than signature-based approaches while remaining computationally tractable. MobSF (Mobile Security Framework) is an open-source framework that performs both static and dynamic analysis of Android (and iOS) applications, producing reports that cover a substantial range of vulnerability categories including insecure data storage, network security, code quality, and manifest misconfiguration. Its API-first architecture has made it widely adopted as a component of automated CI/CD security pipelines, and its integration with existing mobile development workflows has contributed to its uptake in organisations with mature DevSecOps practices. However, MobSF's analytical depth is constrained by its reliance on pre-defined rule sets that do not incorporate contextual knowledge from authoritative security intelligence sources, and its reporting does not include the structured evidence chain required for NRB audit compliance.

[Figure 18 — Literature Review: MobSF Strengths and Limitations]

QARK (Quick Android Review Kit), developed at LinkedIn and released publicly in 2015, focuses specifically on the detection of component exposure vulnerabilities — exported activities, services, broadcast receivers, and content providers that can be accessed without appropriate permission controls. QARK's specialisation in this vulnerability class makes it highly effective for applications with complex inter-component communication architectures, and its output includes proof-of-concept exploit code for identified component exposure vulnerabilities. However, QARK was deprecated in 2019, has not been updated to address Android API levels above 28, does not perform dynamic analysis, and does not cover the majority of OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories. Its false positive rate in the evaluation conducted for this thesis — 42.4% — reflects the limitations of rule-based pattern matching without contextual knowledge enrichment.

[Figure 19 — Literature Review: QARK Strengths and Limitations]

The most recent generation of Android security tools incorporates AI components for vulnerability classification. Kramer et al. (2025) evaluate the application of Large Language Models to vulnerability classification in mobile application contexts, demonstrating that LLMs achieve substantially higher accuracy than traditional machine learning classifiers when provided with appropriate contextual information about the vulnerability's code context and the relevant security standard controls. However, Kramer et al. note that LLM-only classification without RAG context retrieval is subject to hallucination and training data staleness — findings that directly motivate SENTINEL's RAG-LLM integration design. Commercial tools including Checkmarx, Veracode, and NowSecure incorporate proprietary AI triage components that achieve strong detection performance but do not expose the knowledge sources underlying their verdicts, do not provide NRB-compatible evidence packages, and are priced beyond the reach of most Nepalese fintech firms.

The gap that SENTINEL addresses is clear from this survey: no existing open-source or commercially accessible tool provides systematic coverage of all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories, RAG-enriched contextual knowledge retrieval, LLM-assisted verdict generation with full transparency, and a structured proof gate with mandatory human oversight — integrated within a single framework designed to produce NRB-compliant assessment evidence. This gap is the precise space that SENTINEL occupies.

### Behavioural Economics and Security Decision-Making

The behavioural economics literature provides the theoretical framework for understanding why the limitations of existing tools translate into assessment failures even when those tools are available to practitioners. Expected Utility Theory — the normative framework for rational decision-making under uncertainty — predicts that practitioners should evaluate vulnerability alerts based on the probability of each alert representing a genuine finding multiplied by the potential impact of that finding, acting on all alerts where the expected utility of investigation exceeds the expected utility of non-investigation. In practice, as Kahneman and Tversky (1979) demonstrate, human decision-making systematically deviates from Expected Utility Theory in ways that reduce assessment effectiveness.

Kahneman (2011) identifies three cognitive biases with particular relevance to the security assessment context. Loss aversion causes security practitioners to under-investigate ambiguous alerts because the perceived cost of false positive investigation exceeds the perceived benefit of true positive detection — a weighting that produces systematic under-detection of genuine vulnerabilities. In the Nepal fintech context, where assessment teams are small and time-constrained, this bias is particularly potent: practitioners who receive 8,420 raw flags from an automated assessment tool face a loss aversion pressure that causes them to ignore the majority of flags rather than investigating systematically. The availability heuristic causes practitioners to over-weight the risk of vulnerability classes that have featured prominently in recent security incidents — SQL injection, for example, following a high-profile database breach — while under-weighting the risk of less-salient classes such as cryptographic implementation errors or insufficient session management, even when the latter classes are equally or more prevalent in the assessment corpus. Confirmation bias causes analysts who form an early hypothesis about an application's security posture to seek evidence that confirms that hypothesis, under-weighting disconfirming evidence and producing incomplete assessment coverage.

Sarker et al. (2020) provide empirical evidence that alert fatigue — the desensitisation of practitioners to security alerts produced by high-volume automated tools — is a significant contributor to missed detections in real-world security operations, with analysts in high-alert-volume environments detecting substantially fewer true positives than analysts in lower-volume environments despite equal professional competence. This finding is directly relevant to the Kathmandu Valley context, where small security teams managing large application portfolios are particularly vulnerable to the alert volume that undisciplined automated assessment tools produce.

Liao et al. (2013) demonstrate that human-AI collaboration in high-stakes classification tasks outperforms both fully human and fully automated approaches when the AI component provides structured evidence summaries that engage human analytical capacity rather than replacing it. This finding motivates SENTINEL's Level 6 evidence chain presentation design, which ensures that analysts at the mandatory approval gate receive the information required for informed endorsement rather than a binary AI recommendation.

## 14 · Case Studies

### Case Study 1 — MobSF: Mobile Security Framework

#### Implementation and Approach

MobSF (Mobile Security Framework) represents the most widely adopted open-source Android security assessment framework currently available, and its design philosophy — comprehensive static and dynamic analysis delivered through a unified web interface and REST API — has influenced the architectural thinking behind SENTINEL. Originally developed by Ajin Abraham and released under the GPL-3.0 licence, MobSF has been continuously maintained and now supports Android, iOS, and Windows application analysis within a single platform. Its adoption within Kathmandu Valley fintech organisations is limited but growing; at least three major Nepalese digital wallet providers have integrated MobSF into their CI/CD pipelines for automated pre-release security screening, a practice that reflects the broader global trend toward DevSecOps integration of security assessment tooling.

MobSF operates by decompiling submitted APKs using a combination of Androguard, Jadx, and Apktool, extracting the manifest file, source code (both Java and Smali representations), and embedded resources, and then applying a rule set of several hundred predefined patterns to identify potential vulnerabilities. The static analysis component covers manifest misconfiguration, insecure API usage, hardcoded credentials, network security configuration errors, and several other categories. The dynamic analysis component — available when an Android device or emulator is connected — provides runtime behaviour monitoring including network traffic interception, filesystem access logging, and API call tracing.

[Figure 21 — Case Study 1: MobSF Assessment of Nepalese Digital Wallet]

The case study analysis of MobSF's performance on a representative Nepalese digital wallet application reveals both the tool's genuine strengths and its systematic limitations. MobSF successfully identified manifest-level vulnerabilities including exported activities without permission controls, insecure network security configuration permitting cleartext traffic, and hardcoded debug API keys — findings that are directly relevant to the regulatory compliance requirements of NRB IT Guidelines (2012). The API integration capability allowed the digital wallet's development team to receive automated assessment reports as part of their deployment pipeline, reducing the time between code change and security feedback from weeks to minutes.

#### Features and Limitations

MobSF's performance on the 47-APK evaluation corpus produced a detection coverage of 52.7% — meaning that 47.3% of known vulnerabilities in the corpus were not detected — and a false positive rate of 38.1%, meaning that more than one in three findings required analyst investigation to determine that they represented benign code patterns rather than genuine vulnerabilities. The mean assessment time of approximately 17 minutes is acceptable for on-demand assessment but creates pipeline bottlenecks in high-volume evaluation scenarios. The detection gap is attributable to specific architectural limitations: MobSF does not perform JNI (Java Native Interface) analysis, meaning that vulnerabilities in native code libraries are not detected; it does not retrieve contextual knowledge from OWASP MASVS controls during analysis, meaning that its rule set cannot be dynamically enriched with newly published security intelligence; and it does not maintain a multi-level proof gate, meaning that all pattern matches are reported as findings without progressive evidence corroboration.

The 38.1% false positive rate is the most significant operational limitation. In the Nepal fintech context, where small security teams must triage MobSF output manually, a false positive rate of this magnitude produces the alert fatigue documented by Sarker et al. (2020): practitioners confronted with three potentially false alerts for every four true findings develop heuristics for dismissing alerts that simultaneously dismiss genuine findings. The absence of a structured evidence chain for each finding — MobSF reports findings as rule matches without providing OWASP MASVS control references, CWE identifiers, or dynamic confirmation — means that practitioners lack the information required to make rapid and accurate triage decisions.

#### Lessons for SENTINEL

MobSF's API-first architecture validates the design choice to build SENTINEL around a similarly modular, integrable interface — the REST API that SENTINEL exposes for CI/CD pipeline integration is directly informed by MobSF's demonstrated success in this deployment pattern. MobSF's detection gap motivates SENTINEL's RAG enrichment pipeline: by retrieving OWASP MASVS controls and CWE entries relevant to each specific code pattern detected, SENTINEL's agents can identify vulnerability classes that pure rule matching misses. MobSF's inability to analyse native code motivates SENTINEL's Frida 16.x dynamic instrumentation at Levels 4 and 7 of the proof gate, which can observe native library behaviour at runtime independently of source code availability. MobSF's high false positive rate motivates the seven-level proof gate's progressive corroboration requirement, which eliminates the class of spurious pattern matches that constitute the majority of MobSF's false positives.

### Case Study 2 — QARK: Quick Android Review Kit

#### Implementation and Approach

QARK (Quick Android Review Kit) was developed by LinkedIn's security team and publicly released in 2015 as a tool specifically designed to identify component exposure vulnerabilities in Android applications — exported activities, services, broadcast receivers, and content providers that can be accessed by other applications without appropriate permission controls. The LinkedIn origin reflects a specific organisational need: applications deployed within a large enterprise ecosystem face particularly acute risks from component exposure, because a single malicious or misconfigured application within the same device can exploit exported components to exfiltrate data or escalate privileges. In the Kathmandu Valley context, where multiple financial applications are commonly installed on the same device and where the device security posture is not controlled by the financial institution, component exposure represents a significant threat vector for Nepalese banking customers.

QARK operates by decompiling the target APK and performing static analysis of the application's manifest declarations and component implementations, generating a comprehensive inventory of exported components with their associated permission requirements and identifying components where the permission configuration is absent or insufficient. Its output includes proof-of-concept ADB commands and, in some cases, test application APKs that can be used to demonstrate exploitation of identified component exposures — a capability that MobSF does not provide and that is particularly useful for communicating the practical impact of findings to application developers.

[Figure 22 — Case Study 2: QARK Assessment of Kathmandu Banking Application]

The case study analysis of QARK's performance on a Kathmandu banking application revealed its characteristic strengths and limitations. QARK successfully identified three exported activities without permission controls, two exported content providers with path traversal vulnerabilities, and one exported broadcast receiver susceptible to intent spoofing — all genuine high-severity findings that required immediate remediation and that were confirmed by expert manual analysis. The proof-of-concept ADB commands included in QARK's output allowed the banking application's security team to demonstrate the exploitation pathway to senior management, supporting the business case for urgent remediation.

#### Features and Limitations

Despite these genuine contributions, QARK's evaluation performance was substantially below what the Nepalese fintech assessment context requires. Detection coverage across the 47-APK corpus was 41.2%, the lowest of any evaluated approach, because QARK's analysis is effectively confined to component exposure and does not cover the majority of OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories including cryptographic implementation, network security, data storage, or authentication. The false positive rate of 42.4% reflects QARK's pattern matching approach: the tool flags component exports as potentially vulnerable based on configuration patterns without performing the semantic analysis required to determine whether a specific export is actually exploitable in context. In the banking application case study, QARK generated seventeen flags related to a content provider that was intentionally exported for cross-application data sharing with a partner application — a legitimate design choice that QARK's pattern-based analysis could not distinguish from a genuine vulnerability. QARK's deprecation in 2019 means that it does not support Android API levels above 28, rendering it ineffective for assessments of applications targeting contemporary Android versions — a critical limitation for Nepalese banking applications that must support Android 10 and above per NRB technical specifications. The absence of dynamic analysis capability means that QARK cannot confirm whether identified component exposures are actually exploitable under runtime conditions.

#### Lessons for SENTINEL

QARK's high false positive rate — the most dramatic of any evaluated tool — provides the most direct motivation for SENTINEL's seven-level proof gate. The case study reveals that QARK's false positives follow a consistent pattern: they arise from component export configurations that are syntactically suspicious but semantically legitimate, a distinction that static pattern matching cannot make. SENTINEL's Level 3 LLM triage specifically addresses this class of false positive by providing the LLM with the full code context of the flagged component, enabling semantic analysis that distinguishes legitimate from genuinely vulnerable configurations. The Kathmandu banking application case study also revealed a specific vulnerability class — Server-Side Request Forgery in an API client library — that QARK entirely missed because it lay outside the tool's component exposure focus; SENTINEL's multi-specialised agents, covering all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories including network security, would detect this class of vulnerability systematically.

### Case Study 3 — Manual Penetration Testing

#### Implementation and Approach

Manual penetration testing by expert analysts following an OWASP MASVS-guided methodology represents the current gold standard of Android security assessment quality — the approach against which automated tools are judged and which provides the ground truth annotations used in this thesis's evaluation. In the Nepalese fintech context, manual testing is performed by a combination of Nepal-based security consultants (of whom fewer than twelve hold relevant professional certifications (NRB, 2023)) and international consultants engaged on a project basis, typically from India, Singapore, or the United Kingdom. The cost of engaging qualified international consultants for a comprehensive OWASP MASVS-aligned assessment ranges from NPR 150,000 to NPR 300,000 per application, a figure that represents a substantial proportion of the security budget available to smaller Nepalese fintech firms.

The manual testing methodology applied in this case study followed a structured protocol aligned with OWASP MASVS v2.1, encompassing static analysis of decompiled source code using Jadx and Apktool, dynamic analysis using Frida scripting for SSL pinning bypass and runtime value extraction, network traffic interception using Burp Suite, device filesystem examination using ADB, and manual code review of cryptographic implementation and authentication logic. The analyst team comprised two professionals with relevant mobile security certifications, and the assessment was conducted over 7.4 hours across three applications selected from the evaluation corpus.

[Figure 23 — Case Study 3: Manual Penetration Testing vs SENTINEL]

The manual assessment identified a range of high-quality findings including a broken cryptographic implementation using ECB mode for sensitive data encryption, an SSL pinning bypass vulnerability in a certificate validation implementation, an insecure session management flaw that permitted session token reuse after logout, and several data storage issues including unencrypted sensitive data in SharedPreferences. These findings required expert knowledge of Android cryptographic APIs, network security protocols, and session management patterns that exceeds what pattern-matching tools can provide.

#### Features and Limitations

Manual testing achieved 53.4% detection coverage across the evaluation corpus — a figure that is counterintuitively low for an expert-led process, but which reflects the time constraints under which the assessment was conducted. At 7.4 hours per application, a complete assessment of the 47-APK corpus would require approximately 348 hours of expert analyst time — economically prohibitive for any Nepalese fintech firm and practically impossible given the scarcity of qualified practitioners. The 31.2% false positive rate, while substantially lower than either MobSF or QARK, reflects the genuine difficulty of distinguishing certain classes of implementation pattern from vulnerabilities without access to the application's full development context. The NPR 150,000–300,000 per assessment cost places comprehensive manual testing beyond the reach of smaller fintech operators, creating a two-tier security landscape in Nepal where larger institutions can afford adequate assessment and smaller institutions cannot.

The irreproducibility of manual testing is an additional limitation with direct regulatory implications. NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023) require that assessment evidence be sufficient to support regulatory examination, but manual testing produces findings whose evidential basis depends on the individual analyst's knowledge, experience, and documentation discipline. Two different analysts assessing the same application may produce findings that differ both in content and in the level of evidence documentation, making it difficult for NRB to conduct consistent cross-organisation comparisons.

#### Lessons for SENTINEL

The manual testing case study provides two critical lessons for SENTINEL's design. First, it validates that analyst-grade security findings — comparable in quality to those produced by expert penetration testers — are achievable through systematic, thorough analysis: the findings generated by manual testing are the quality benchmark that SENTINEL aspires to match or exceed, and the 94.3% detection coverage achieved by SENTINEL against the 53.4% achieved by manual testing confirms that systematic multi-specialised agent orchestration across all OWASP MASVS categories can exceed expert human coverage. Second, it demonstrates that the bottleneck in manual testing is not analytical quality but analytical capacity: expert analysts are capable of high-quality findings but cannot cover all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories completely within realistic time and cost constraints. SENTINEL's systematic category coverage advantage — the ability to dispatch multi-specialised agents across all fourteen categories simultaneously — is the primary architectural response to this bottleneck.

### Case Study 4 — Commercial AI-Assisted Security Tools

#### Implementation and Approach

The commercial AI-assisted security tool market is represented in this case study by three platforms that have achieved significant enterprise adoption: Checkmarx One, Veracode, and NowSecure. Each platform incorporates AI components for vulnerability triage and prioritisation, achieves broader OWASP MASVS category coverage than open-source alternatives, and provides structured reporting aligned with enterprise risk management requirements. Their analysis is relevant to SENTINEL's design because they represent the state of the art in commercially deployed AI-assisted Android security assessment and therefore define the performance ceiling against which SENTINEL's open-source approach must be evaluated.

Checkmarx One combines static application security testing (SAST), software composition analysis (SCA), and API security testing within a unified platform, applying machine learning models to vulnerability prioritisation that have been trained on large corpora of enterprise application findings. Veracode provides both static and dynamic analysis with AI-powered triage that identifies high-confidence findings and suppresses low-confidence ones, achieving false positive rates reported in vendor documentation as substantially below those of open-source alternatives. NowSecure specialises specifically in mobile application security, providing OWASP MASVS-aligned assessment with automated dynamic analysis and a reporting format aligned with major compliance frameworks.

[Figure 24 — Case Study 4: Commercial AI Security Tools vs SENTINEL]

The case study analysis of commercial tool capabilities relevant to the Kathmandu Valley context reveals a significant capability gap between what commercial platforms offer and what is accessible to Nepalese fintech organisations. Commercial platform licensing costs range from USD 10,000 to USD 50,000+ per year for enterprise deployments, with mobile-specific assessment capabilities typically requiring additional licensing. The proprietary nature of the AI triage components means that practitioners and regulators cannot examine the knowledge sources underlying vulnerability verdicts, a critical deficiency in the NRB audit compliance context where evidence traceability is explicitly required. None of the evaluated commercial platforms provides evidence packages formatted to the specific requirements of NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023), requiring additional post-processing and reformatting that adds cost and introduces potential for documentation errors.

#### Features and Limitations

Commercial AI-assisted platforms achieve detection coverage rates that are substantially higher than open-source alternatives, reflecting the advantage of proprietary training corpora, sustained engineering investment, and large enterprise client bases that generate the feedback data required to improve AI triage models continuously. Vendor-reported false positive rates are substantially lower than those achieved by MobSF or QARK, consistent with the investment in AI triage that reduces the pattern-matching false positives characteristic of rule-based tools. Assessment throughput is high, with cloud-based infrastructure providing computational resources that exceed what most Nepalese fintech organisations could deploy locally.

The limitations of commercial platforms in the Nepal context are, however, substantial and in some respects disqualifying. Cost is the most immediate: USD 10,000+ annual licensing represents a prohibitive expenditure for most Nepalese fintech firms, which typically operate with total annual IT security budgets of NPR 1 million to NPR 5 million. Opacity is equally significant: the inability to examine the evidential basis of AI triage verdicts is incompatible with NRB's requirement for auditable assessment evidence, and practitioners who cannot understand why a tool produced a particular verdict cannot make informed decisions about whether to endorse that verdict. The absence of NRB-compatible evidence packaging means that commercial tool output cannot be submitted directly to NRB examination without significant additional work, eroding the time and cost efficiency advantages that commercial tools otherwise provide.

#### Lessons for SENTINEL

The commercial platform case study validates two foundational design choices in SENTINEL's architecture. First, it confirms that AI triage is effective at reducing false positive rates below what pattern-based tools achieve, motivating SENTINEL's LLM triage component at Level 3 of the proof gate. Second, it demonstrates that opacity and cost are the primary barriers to commercial platform adoption in the Nepal fintech context, motivating SENTINEL's commitment to open-source MIT-licenced release, full algorithmic transparency through the verdict record, and NRB-aligned evidence packaging. SENTINEL does not attempt to replicate the full engineering investment of commercial platforms but rather to provide the capabilities that are most critical for the Nepalese context — NRB compliance, cost accessibility, and algorithmic transparency — at the expense of the enterprise-scale features that Nepalese fintech firms do not require.

## 15 · Literature Review Synthesis

The four case studies reviewed in Section 14 converge on a single central finding: no existing tool — open-source, commercial, or manual — addresses the complete OWASP MASVS vulnerability lifecycle in a manner that simultaneously achieves high detection coverage, acceptable false positive rates, regulatory evidence compliance, and economic accessibility in the Nepalese fintech context. This synthesis is not merely a negative finding — a catalogue of gaps — but a positive specification of what a framework addressing those gaps must provide, and SENTINEL's architectural design can be read directly from this specification.

The Watson cautionary lesson — drawn from the history of AI in chess, where the initial claim that AI would "never" beat grandmasters was succeeded by the realisation that AI assistance changes the nature of optimal human strategy rather than simply replacing human players — applies directly to the Nepal cybersecurity context. The lesson is not that AI-assisted security assessment will make human practitioners obsolete, but that the optimal deployment of AI assistance requires a fundamentally different practitioner role: less pattern-matching and alert triage, more judgement-intensive review of AI-assembled evidence. SENTINEL is designed to support precisely this role transition, concentrating human analytical effort at Level 6 where it is most valuable rather than distributing it across the full volume of raw detection outputs where it is most subject to fatigue and cognitive bias.

Four direct framework connections emerge from the synthesis. First, MobSF's high false positive rate and absence of contextual knowledge enrichment motivate SENTINEL's RAG pipeline: by retrieving OWASP MASVS controls and CWE entries relevant to each flagged code pattern, SENTINEL provides the contextual grounding that distinguishes genuine vulnerabilities from benign implementations of similar patterns. Second, QARK's catastrophic false positive rate of 42.4% and its pattern-matching architecture directly motivate SENTINEL's seven-level proof gate: the proof gate's progressive corroboration requirement eliminates the class of pattern-matching false positives that constitutes QARK's primary limitation. Third, manual testing's 53.4% coverage ceiling despite expert analyst involvement motivates SENTINEL's multi-specialised agent category completeness: by deploying dedicated agents across all fourteen OWASP MASVS categories simultaneously, SENTINEL achieves the systematic coverage that individual expert analysts cannot within realistic time constraints. Fourth, commercial platforms' opacity and cost limitations directly motivate SENTINEL's open-source, auditable design and NRB evidence packaging: the specific barriers that prevent commercial platform adoption in Nepal are precisely the barriers that SENTINEL's design choices eliminate.

The synthesis also reveals a cross-domain validation pattern that strengthens the theoretical case for SENTINEL's architecture. RAG-LLM integration has demonstrated effectiveness in legal document analysis (Gao et al., 2023), medical diagnosis support (Lewis et al., 2020), and financial instrument classification — all domains characterised by a structured authoritative knowledge base, high-stakes decisions, and the need for evidence-grounded verdicts. ## 16 · Integration of Tools and Technologies in Framework Design and Development

### Overview and Orchestration Challenge

The central engineering challenge in SENTINEL's design is the orchestration of multiple heterogeneous tools and services — static decompilers, dynamic instrumentation frameworks, vector databases, LLM APIs, relational databases, and object stores — into a coherent, reliable, and auditable assessment pipeline that produces consistent outputs across diverse APK inputs. This challenge is amplified in the Nepalese fintech context by three constraints: the computational resource limitations of Kathmandu Valley security infrastructure; the variable network connectivity that affects cloud service availability; and the strict NRB evidence packaging requirements that demand specific output formats regardless of the internal complexity of the assessment process. SENTINEL's architecture addresses each of these constraints through specific design choices documented in this section.

[Figure 25 — SENTINEL Overall Architecture]

### Ten-Phase Assessment Pipeline

SENTINEL's assessment pipeline comprises ten sequential phases that transform a raw APK submission into a structured, NRB-compliant assessment report. Each phase has defined inputs, outputs, and failure modes, and each phase's execution is logged to the PostgreSQL audit trail to support post-assessment examination of the assessment process itself.

**Phase 1 — APK Ingestion and Authorisation Gate:** The assessment begins when an APK is submitted via the REST API or command-line interface. The framework computes a SHA-256 hash of the APK binary and checks the hash against a deduplication database to avoid re-assessing previously assessed applications when the binary has not changed since the last assessment. A Docker container is instantiated specifically for this assessment, providing the per-scan isolation required by the privacy architecture. The authorisation gate validates the submitted authorisation form against the required fields; assessment proceeds only if a valid, complete form is present. This gate achieves a 100% rejection rate for unauthorised submissions.

**Phase 2 — Manifest Analysis:** The APK manifest (AndroidManifest.xml) is extracted and analysed using Androguard, which provides programmatic access to all manifest declarations including application permissions, component declarations (activities, services, receivers, providers), intent filter configurations, and exported component flags. The manifest analysis agent enumerates all permissions requested, flags dangerous permissions with no apparent functional justification, identifies all exported components without permission controls, and records the minimum and target SDK versions for compatibility analysis. Manifest findings are stored as preliminary evidence items that may progress through the proof gate if corroborated.

**Phase 3 — Static Code Analysis:** The APK is decompiled using Jadx (for Java/Kotlin source reconstruction) and Apktool (for Smali bytecode and resource extraction). Multi-specialised agents are dispatched for each of the fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories, each operating with a category-specific pattern library and payload set developed through analysis of the OWASP MASVS v2.1 control specifications. Agents operate concurrently within the Docker container, with results collected asynchronously as agents complete their analysis. The concurrent dispatch architecture ensures that assessment time is not additive across categories, maintaining the 8.3-minute mean assessment time despite the comprehensive category coverage.

**Phase 4 — RAG Enrichment:** For each potential finding identified in Phase 3, the RAG pipeline is invoked to retrieve contextually relevant knowledge from the ChromaDB corpus. The finding's code context and vulnerability category are encoded by the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer into a 384-dimensional embedding vector, which is used to query ChromaDB by cosine similarity for the top four most semantically similar passages from the 2,323-item corpus. The retrieved passages — which include specific OWASP MASVS controls, CWE entries, and OSV advisories — are assembled into a structured context block that augments the subsequent LLM prompt. Retrieval latency of 12 to 18 milliseconds per query imposes negligible overhead on the assessment timeline.

**Phase 5 — LLM Triage:** The augmented prompt — comprising the code context, vulnerability hypothesis, and RAG-retrieved knowledge block — is submitted to the LLM triage router. The router attempts Groq Llama 3.3 70B first, falling back to Cerebras and then to Ollama qwen2.5-coder:7b if the primary provider is unavailable. Temperature is set to 0.0 for all providers, ensuring deterministic output. The LLM returns a structured JSON verdict containing the vulnerability category, confidence score (0.0–1.0), CVSS v3.1 base score, supporting OWASP MASVS controls and CWE identifiers, and a natural language explanation. A four-shape JSON tolerance parser handles the minor format variations across provider implementations without requiring post-processing by the analyst. Findings with LLM confidence scores below 0.7 are flagged for additional dynamic confirmation rather than being escalated directly.

**Phase 6 — Dynamic Setup:** For findings that have passed Level 3 of the proof gate (LLM confidence ≥ 0.7), the framework initiates dynamic analysis setup. An ADB device lease is acquired, connecting to an Android device (physical or emulated) designated for this assessment. Frida 16.x server binary is pushed to the device and activated, establishing the instrumentation channel. The setup phase includes validation that the Frida server is responsive before any instrumentation scripts are executed, preventing the ADB race conditions that were identified as a technical challenge during Sprint 2.

**Phase 7 — Runtime Confirmation:** Frida 16.x hooks are deployed to intercept the specific API calls and code paths implicated in the static analysis findings. For cryptographic vulnerability findings, Frida hooks intercept cipher instantiation calls to confirm the use of weak algorithms. For network security findings, Frida hooks intercept SSL/TLS connection establishment to confirm certificate validation behaviour. For storage security findings, Frida hooks monitor file system write operations to confirm unencrypted data persistence. Runtime confirmation produces Level 4 evidence — dynamic instrumentation artefacts that provide independent confirmation of static analysis findings. Findings that are dynamically confirmed are escalated; findings that cannot be confirmed dynamically within the instrumentation window are retained at Level 3 pending further investigation.

**Phase 8 — Evidence Assembly:** Confirmed findings and their associated evidence artefacts are assembled into structured evidence records and stored in the three-tier memory architecture. The Redis hot cache maintains assessment state and active finding records with a 24-hour TTL, supporting the 47% cache hit rate observed in the evaluation. PostgreSQL warm storage maintains the permanent audit trail including all LLM verdict records, analyst endorsement decisions, and phase completion timestamps. MinIO cold storage archives the complete forensic artefacts — Frida capture logs, APK binary copies, decompiled source archives, and generated reports — in the format required for NRB long-term retention.

**Phase 9 — Human Approval Gate:** All findings that have achieved Level 5 evidence storage are presented to the designated analyst through the SENTINEL web interface or API. The analyst reviews the full evidence chain — static detection pattern, multi-agent corroboration record, LLM verdict with confidence score and supporting references, and Frida dynamic confirmation artefacts — and makes an explicit endorsement or rejection decision. Endorsement is recorded in PostgreSQL with the analyst identifier, timestamp, and decision rationale. No finding can achieve CONFIRMED status without a recorded endorsement. During the evaluation period, 82.4% of Level 5 findings were endorsed, 11.3% were rejected as false positives, and 6.3% were returned for additional evidence collection before a final decision was made.

**Phase 10 — Report Generation:** Confirmed findings are assembled into the final assessment report in PDF and JSON formats, with each finding including its CVSS v3.1 score, OWASP MASVS control references, CWE identifier, natural language description, remediation guidance, and the full evidence chain record required for NRB audit compliance. The JSON format supports integration with issue tracking systems and risk management platforms. The NRB evidence package — including the authorisation form, assessment execution log, finding records, and LLM verdict transparency records — is assembled and archived in MinIO for regulatory retention.

[Figure 26 — Ten-Phase Assessment Pipeline]

### Multi-Specialised Agent Architecture

SENTINEL's multi-specialised agent architecture is the component most directly responsible for its superior detection coverage compared to existing single-method tools. By deploying dedicated agent clusters for each of the fourteen OWASP MASVS vulnerability categories, SENTINEL ensures that no category is neglected due to practitioner preference, time pressure, or cognitive salience effects. Each agent cluster operates with a category-specific pattern library developed from OWASP MASVS v2.1 control specifications, a category-specific payload set for dynamic testing, and category-specific RAG query templates that retrieve the most relevant knowledge from the ChromaDB corpus.

[Figure 27 — Fourteen OWASP MASVS Vulnerability Categories]

The scikit-learn GradientBoostingClassifier (n_estimators=50, max_depth=3) is used exclusively for agent retry strategy selection — determining, when an agent fails to produce a result within its timeout window, whether to retry the same analysis approach, escalate to a different agent variant, or defer the finding for analyst review. This use is deliberately narrow: the classifier is not used for vulnerability classification (which is the LLM's role) but for the operational optimisation of agent resource allocation, a context in which the classifier's tendency toward conservative predictions is an asset rather than a limitation.

### Three-Tier Memory Architecture

SENTINEL's three-tier memory architecture is designed to satisfy three simultaneous requirements that are in tension with each other: the sub-millisecond access latency required for real-time agent coordination during assessment; the ACID compliance and query capability required for audit trail management; and the long-term retention capacity and cost-effectiveness required for NRB evidence archiving.

[Figure 28 — Three-Tier Memory Architecture]

Redis serves as the hot tier, providing in-memory storage for active assessment state, agent coordination signals, and recently accessed assessment records. The 24-hour time-to-live configuration ensures that transient assessment data is automatically expired without manual management, supporting the privacy architecture's data minimisation requirement. The 47% cache hit rate observed during evaluation demonstrates that the Redis caching layer materially reduces LLM API calls and ChromaDB query load, contributing to the sub-10-minute assessment time. PostgreSQL serves as the warm tier, providing relational storage for the permanent audit trail with full ACID compliance. All analyst endorsement records, LLM verdict transparency records, and phase completion logs are stored in PostgreSQL with appropriate indexing for NRB audit query patterns. MinIO serves as the cold tier, providing S3-compatible object storage for the large binary artefacts — Frida capture logs, APK archives, and generated reports — that must be retained for NRB compliance but are accessed infrequently after the initial assessment cycle.

### RAG Knowledge Base

The RAG knowledge base is the component that most directly addresses the contextual knowledge limitation of pattern-based tools. Its 2,323 passages are drawn from three authoritative sources that together provide comprehensive coverage of the OWASP MASVS control framework: 273 control specifications from OWASP MASVS v2.1 that provide the normative security requirements against which findings are evaluated; 1,200 CWE entries that provide the weakness classification taxonomy linking code patterns to vulnerability categories; and 850 OSV advisories that provide current vulnerability intelligence including published CVEs affecting Android libraries and frameworks in common use across the Kathmandu Valley fintech ecosystem.

[Figure 29 — ChromaDB RAG Embeddings Detail]

The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer encodes each passage into a 384-dimensional vector during the initial corpus construction phase, which is performed once during deployment and updated incrementally as new OWASP MASVS versions, CWE entries, or OSV advisories are published. Cosine similarity is used for retrieval because it is invariant to passage length, making it appropriate for a corpus that includes both short CWE entry descriptions and lengthy OWASP MASVS control specifications. The choice of top-4 retrieval reflects a deliberate optimisation: retrieving fewer than four passages risks missing relevant context; retrieving more than four passages increases prompt length and LLM inference cost without proportionate benefit to verdict quality, as measured by the ablation experiments conducted during Sprint 2.

### LLM Triage Router

The LLM triage router implements a priority chain that ensures assessment continuity under variable network conditions. The router first attempts Groq Llama 3.3 70B, which provides the highest-quality verdicts due to the model's size and the Groq hardware's fast inference. If the Groq API is unavailable or returns an error, the router falls back to Cerebras, which provides an alternative cloud inference path. If both cloud providers are unavailable, the router falls back to Ollama running qwen2.5-coder:7b locally, ensuring that assessment can continue even without internet connectivity — a design choice motivated by the documented connectivity variability of the Kathmandu Valley internet infrastructure.

[Figure 30 — LLM Router and Redis Caching]

The temperature 0.0 setting applied to all three providers ensures that LLM outputs are deterministic: the same vulnerability evidence, submitted on different occasions, will produce the same structured verdict. This determinism is essential for audit trail integrity — the LLM verdict recorded in PostgreSQL must be reproducible by any subsequent audit process that applies the same prompt to the same model with the same settings. The four-shape JSON tolerance parser addresses the practical reality that LLM providers return JSON output with minor format variations (differing whitespace, key ordering, and nested structure) that must be normalised before downstream pipeline stages can process the verdict.

### Seven-Level Proof Gate

The seven-level proof gate is SENTINEL's most distinctive architectural component, and the one most directly motivated by the Kahneman and Tversky (1979) loss aversion analysis described in Section 03. By requiring progressive escalation through seven levels of evidence before a finding achieves CONFIRMED status, the proof gate addresses the two primary failure modes of existing assessment tools: raw pattern matching that produces excessive false positives, and human analyst over-triage that causes genuine findings to be dismissed due to alert fatigue.

[Figure 31 — Seven-Level Proof Gate Escalation Flow]

Level 1 (Pattern Detection) is passed by any finding where a static analysis agent identifies a code pattern matching a known vulnerability signature from the category-specific pattern library. Level 2 (Corroboration) requires that at least two independent agents, operating on different analysis vectors (for example, source code analysis and manifest analysis, or bytecode analysis and string literal analysis), have both flagged the same vulnerability hypothesis. Level 3 (LLM Verdict) requires that the LLM triage router returns a structured verdict with a confidence score of 0.7 or above, grounded in the RAG-retrieved knowledge context. Level 4 (Frida Runtime Confirmation) requires that dynamic instrumentation has confirmed the vulnerability in runtime application behaviour, providing evidence independent of static code analysis. Level 5 (Evidence Storage) records all evidence artefacts in the three-tier memory architecture, creating the permanent audit record. Level 6 (Human Approval) — mandatory for CONFIRMED status — requires explicit analyst endorsement after review of the complete evidence chain. Level 7 (PoC Generation) is activated only post-endorsement for in-scope assessments, producing read-only exploit pathway documentation.

The funnel effect of the proof gate is dramatic: 8,420 raw Level 1 flags are reduced through progressive evidence requirements to 534 CONFIRMED vulnerabilities at Level 6. This 6.3% retention rate reflects the aggressive false positive suppression achieved by the combination of multi-agent corroboration, LLM semantic analysis, and dynamic confirmation — each level eliminating a different class of false positive rather than filtering redundantly on the same signal. The 8.3% false positive rate of the final CONFIRMED findings reflects the irreducible residual of false positives that pass all seven levels, representing the boundary of what structured evidence accumulation can achieve without analyst judgement.

### Technical Challenges

Three technical challenges were encountered and resolved during the six-month development cycle. APK obfuscation using tools such as ProGuard and DexGuard renames classes, methods, and variables to meaningless identifiers, defeating string-matching-based pattern detection. SENTINEL's response to obfuscation combines Smali bytecode analysis (which is resistant to identifier renaming), behavioural fingerprinting through Frida hooks (which observes what code does rather than what it is called), and semantic embedding matching through ChromaDB (which represents code semantics independently of identifier names). JSON output format variation across LLM providers required the development of the four-shape JSON tolerance parser, which normalises provider-specific formatting differences before downstream processing. ADB race conditions during dynamic setup — where the Frida server process was not fully initialised before instrumentation scripts were dispatched — required the implementation of a readiness polling mechanism with exponential backoff, eliminating the race condition at the cost of a small additional setup time.

## 17 · Ethical Reflection in Development

Ethical reflection in SENTINEL's development was not confined to a pre-submission review phase but was integrated as a parallel design criterion throughout all three agile development sprints. This integration is reflected in the composition of each sprint review: alongside technical milestone achievements, each sprint review included a structured ethical checkpoint assessing the ethical implications of components developed during that sprint and documenting any ethical issues identified and the design responses adopted. The Agile sprint structure proved conducive to this iterative ethical integration, allowing ethical concerns surfaced during implementation to be addressed in subsequent sprints rather than being deferred to a post-completion review where remediation would be costly.

The authorisation gate's status as a hard architectural constraint rather than a policy recommendation reflects the most fundamental ethical design decision in SENTINEL's development. During Sprint 1, two alternative implementations of the authorisation check were considered: a soft warning that flagged unauthorised submissions but permitted assessment to proceed, and a hard gate that rejected unauthorised submissions unconditionally. The soft warning approach was rejected on the grounds that it would make SENTINEL legally liable for facilitating unauthorised security testing — a criminal offence under Nepal's Electronic Transactions Act and equivalent legislation in jurisdictions where SENTINEL might be deployed — and that it would undermine the ethical credibility of a framework that positions human oversight as a core governance principle. A framework that warns but does not enforce authorisation requirements communicates that authorisation is optional; only a framework that enforces them absolutely communicates that authorisation is a non-negotiable precondition for responsible assessment.

The privacy architecture was stress-tested during Sprint 2 through structured attempts to extract data across assessment boundaries within the Docker container environment. These tests confirmed that the per-scan Docker isolation successfully prevents cross-assessment data leakage: an assessment conducted immediately after a previous assessment of a different application found no residual data from the previous assessment in its environment. The PII marker detection mechanism was validated against a test set of Nepalese financial application strings, including Nepali-script names, identity card number formats (seven-digit national identity numbers and nine-digit citizenship numbers), and NPR-denominated account balance strings. Detection accuracy for these PII categories was 91.3% — a figure that is high but not perfect, and that is explicitly documented in the assessment report as a limitation.

The algorithmic transparency record was designed in Sprint 1 and refined in Sprint 3 following consultation with NRB audit practitioners. The initial design recorded the model identifier and temperature but did not include the RAG passage identifiers or prompt hash. Feedback from the audit practitioners indicated that the missing fields were essential for regulatory examination: without the RAG passage identifiers, an auditor could not verify that the retrieved knowledge context was appropriate to the vulnerability being assessed; without the prompt hash, an auditor could not confirm that the LLM prompt had not been modified between assessment and reporting. The refined design, which includes all five transparency record fields, was confirmed by three NRB-experienced professionals as sufficient for regulatory audit purposes.

A statistically significant bias was discovered during Sprint 3 evaluation and is documented here in the spirit of honest academic reporting: SENTINEL's agents exhibited a higher false positive rate on applications that included Nepali-language string literals than on applications whose string resources were entirely in English or Latin-script transliterated Nepali. Investigation identified the cause as the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model's underrepresentation of Nepali-script text in its training corpus, causing Nepali strings to receive lower semantic similarity scores than semantically equivalent Latin-script strings and occasionally triggering false hardcoded credential detections when Nepali-script text was misclassified as an encoded secret. This bias was mitigated in the final evaluation through explicit Nepali-script exclusion rules in the credential detection pattern library, but the root cause — embedding model training data composition — requires a longer-term solution through fine-tuning on Nepali-language security corpora. This finding is documented as a limitation in Section 18 and as a future work item in Section 19.

The Level 6 human approval data collected during the evaluation period provides rich insight into the governance model's operational performance. Of all findings presented to the analyst panel, 82.4% were endorsed as CONFIRMED, 11.3% were rejected as false positives (findings where the analyst determined that the LLM's confidence score was misplaced despite the evidence chain), and 6.3% were returned for additional evidence collection — typically requests for Frida confirmation on findings where dynamic analysis had been inconclusive. This distribution is consistent with the design intent: a high endorsement rate demonstrates that the proof gate is presenting findings of sufficient quality for analyst confidence, while the 11.3% rejection rate confirms that the mandatory human oversight is providing genuine value rather than rubber-stamping AI verdicts. The 6.3% evidence collection requests demonstrate that the analyst interface successfully communicates what additional evidence would be required to resolve uncertainty.

The overreliance risk — the concern that analysts who consistently endorse high-quality AI evidence chains will progressively reduce their independent critical engagement with findings — was identified as an ethical concern during Sprint 2 following observation of analyst behaviour in simulated assessment sessions. Analysts given a simple "endorse/reject" interface endorsed more quickly and with less stated rationale than analysts given an interface that required engagement with each element of the evidence chain before endorsement. The final Level 6 interface design requires analysts to document the specific elements of the evidence chain on which their endorsement decision is based, a requirement that maintains analytical engagement and provides a documented basis for the endorsement decision that can be examined during NRB audit.

GDPR Article 5(1)(c) data minimisation compliance was formally assessed during Sprint 3 against a structured checklist derived from the Article 29 Working Party guidance on data minimisation in automated processing. The assessment confirmed that SENTINEL collects only the data necessary for the specified assessment purpose, retains it only for the period required by NRB retention specifications, and processes it within the Docker-isolated environment that prevents incidental data exposure. The formal assessment record is included in the NRB evidence package for each assessment.

NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023) compliance was assessed against the specific requirements of those guidelines, confirming that SENTINEL's evidence packaging, audit trail maintenance, and reporting format satisfy the documented NRB specifications. The compliance assessment involved review by a Nepalese legal practitioner specialising in financial regulation and confirmed that no modifications to SENTINEL's evidence package format are required for NRB submission.

## 18 · Findings

### Research Question 1 — Technical Findings

The findings confirm that a multi-specialised agent framework integrating Retrieval-Augmented Generation knowledge retrieval and Large Language Model triage can be designed, implemented, and deployed to achieve statistically significant improvements in Android APK vulnerability detection coverage and false positive rates compared to existing single-method automated assessment tools, when evaluated across a corpus of real-world applications including Nepalese fintech APKs. This direct answer to RQ1 is supported by a comprehensive body of quantitative evidence that is presented in the following paragraphs.

SENTINEL achieved a vulnerability detection coverage of 94.3% across the 47-APK evaluation corpus, compared to 52.7% for MobSF, 41.2% for QARK, and 53.4% for manual expert testing. The improvement over MobSF is 41.6 percentage points; over QARK, 53.1 percentage points; and over manual testing, 40.9 percentage points. These improvements are consistent across application categories and are not attributable to a single application type or vulnerability class. The superior coverage is most pronounced in the categories where existing tools are weakest: JNI and native library analysis (where MobSF provides no coverage and SENTINEL achieves 87.4% coverage through Frida dynamic instrumentation); cryptographic implementation analysis (where QARK provides no coverage and SENTINEL achieves 91.2% coverage through LLM-assisted semantic analysis of cipher configurations); and session management analysis (where manual testing achieves high coverage within time constraints but SENTINEL achieves comparable coverage systematically and reproducibly).

The false positive rate of 8.3% represents a reduction of 29.8 percentage points compared to MobSF (38.1%), 34.1 percentage points compared to QARK (42.4%), and 22.9 percentage points compared to manual testing (31.2%). The false positive rate reduction is the architectural achievement of the seven-level proof gate: the 8,420 raw Level 1 pattern detection flags are reduced through progressive evidence corroboration to 534 CONFIRMED findings at Level 6, of which 44 are subsequently determined to be false positives through ground truth annotation review. The proof gate funnel demonstrates that the majority of false positives arise from Level 1 pattern matching — the same mechanism that accounts for the high false positive rates of both MobSF and QARK — and that each subsequent proof gate level eliminates a specific and distinct class of false positive rather than redundantly filtering the same signal.

The mean assessment time of 8.3 minutes represents a reduction of approximately 98% compared to the 7.4-hour mean time for manual expert testing, and an improvement of approximately 50% compared to the 17-minute mean time for MobSF. The 8.3-minute time includes all ten pipeline phases from APK submission to report generation, and reflects the efficiency of the concurrent multi-specialised agent dispatch architecture. This throughput enables a Nepalese fintech organisation with a small security team to conduct OWASP MASVS-aligned assessments of its complete application portfolio on a regular basis rather than the sporadic, resource-constrained assessments that current practice permits.

The 84% expert-agreement equivalent — derived from the 82.4% analyst endorsement rate at Level 6 combined with the quality of endorsed findings — provides a measure of SENTINEL's output quality relative to human expert judgement. This metric does not directly compare to the formal expert agreement rates reported in the baseline comparison, because the evaluation methodology differs, but it confirms that SENTINEL's CONFIRMED findings are of sufficient quality to withstand scrutiny by qualified security professionals.

H1 is confirmed with a substantial margin above both specified thresholds: detection coverage of 94.3% exceeds the 80% threshold by 14.3 percentage points, and the false positive rate of 8.3% is below the 15% threshold by 6.7 percentage points. The improvements over all three baseline comparators are consistent and substantial, and the improvement magnitude is consistent across application categories.

The limitations of these results warrant honest documentation. The 47-APK evaluation corpus, while representative of the Nepalese fintech context, is not sufficient to establish universal performance claims across all Android application categories; applications with particularly aggressive obfuscation or unusual architecture may produce different coverage profiles. The computational requirements of SENTINEL — ChromaDB vector operations, LLM API calls, and Frida dynamic instrumentation — demand infrastructure that is at the higher end of what Nepalese fintech organisations can currently deploy, and the assessment time of 8.3 minutes may increase for very large APKs with extensive native code components. The Nepali-script string literal bias documented in Section 17 represents a known limitation of the current embedding model configuration that affects false positive rates for applications with Nepali-language user interface resources.

### Research Question 2 — Ethical Findings

The ethical governance audit confirms that SENTINEL's governance architecture successfully mitigates the principal ethical risks of AI-assisted security assessment and satisfies the compliance requirements of GDPR Article 5 and NRB Cyber Resilience Guidelines (2023). This direct answer to RQ2 is supported by the structured audit findings documented in this subsection.

The authorisation gate's performance was unambiguous: 100% of submissions without valid authorisation forms were rejected without exception during the evaluation period, including test submissions made deliberately without authorisation to verify gate function. This performance confirms that the authorisation gate functions as a genuine hard constraint rather than an advisory warning, satisfying the primary ethical requirement that automated security assessment cannot be initiated without explicit authorisation.

The privacy-preserving analysis mechanisms were validated through cross-assessment boundary testing and PII marker detection accuracy measurement. The 91.3% PII detection accuracy for Nepalese financial application string formats is high but below 100%, and this limitation is documented honestly in every assessment report. The per-scan Docker isolation successfully prevented all tested attempts at cross-assessment data leakage.

The algorithmic transparency record was confirmed by three NRB-experienced professionals as sufficient for regulatory audit purposes, providing the level of evidential traceability required for NRB examination of assessment methodology. The full transparency record — model identifier, temperature, prompt hash, RAG passage identifiers, and structured LLM verdict — provides a complete and reproducible account of every AI-assisted classification decision in the assessment pipeline.

The human oversight gate operated as specified: the 82.4% endorsement rate, 11.3% rejection rate, and 6.3% evidence collection request rate demonstrate that the Level 6 interface is generating genuine analyst engagement rather than passive approval of AI verdicts. The overreliance risk identified in Sprint 2 was successfully mitigated by the evidence chain engagement requirement in the final Level 6 interface design.

H2 is confirmed in its conditional form: SENTINEL's automation substantially enhances detection coverage and reduces assessment time, but the ethical governance model is demonstrably necessary rather than optional. The authorisation gate, privacy controls, transparency record, and mandatory human approval together constitute a governance architecture that converts an inherently risky automated capability into a responsibly deployable assessment framework aligned with both international data protection standards and Nepal-specific regulatory requirements.

## 19 · Future Works

The evaluation results and the ethical reflection documented in Sections 17 and 18 identify several directions in which SENTINEL's capabilities can be extended in future research and development cycles. These future works represent honest responses to the limitations of the current implementation rather than speculative aspirations disconnected from SENTINEL's actual performance profile.

The most significant technical extension is the addition of iOS IPA support. Frida 16.x is fully compatible with iOS instrumentation, and the OWASP MASVS framework applies to iOS as well as Android; extending SENTINEL's static analysis components to support IPA decompilation using tools such as class-dump and Hopper Disassembler would enable the framework to address the complete mobile application security assessment scope, which is particularly relevant for Nepalese fintech firms that deploy both Android and iOS versions of their applications. The multi-specialised agent architecture would require category-specific adaptation for the iOS API surface, but the core RAG, LLM, proof gate, and governance components would require minimal modification.

The development of a Nepal fintech-specific OWASP MASVS profile — a curated subset and extension of OWASP MASVS v2.1 controls calibrated to the specific threat landscape, regulatory requirements, and common implementation patterns of Nepalese fintech applications — would improve both detection coverage and false positive rates for the target deployment context. This profile would incorporate NRB API interaction patterns specific to the Nepal interbank payment infrastructure, Nepali-language user interface string processing guidance (addressing the bias documented in Section 17), and control extensions reflecting emerging threats in the Nepal cybersecurity landscape. Development of this profile in partnership with Tribhuvan University's Computer Science department and NRB's IT department would ensure that it reflects both academic rigour and regulatory authority.

Federated learning for the scikit-learn GradientBoostingClassifier would address the training data diversity limitation identified in Section 18. By enabling multiple SENTINEL deployments at different Nepalese fintech organisations to contribute anonymised agent retry outcome data to a shared model training process without sharing raw assessment data, federated learning would improve the classifier's generalisation to diverse application architectures and obfuscation strategies while preserving the data minimisation principles of the privacy architecture.

An analyst engagement monitoring module — tracking metrics such as time spent reviewing each evidence chain element, decision reversal patterns, and endorsement rationale completeness — would provide operational intelligence about overreliance risk that the current implementation does not measure. If monitoring revealed that endorsement rationale quality was declining over time, suggesting that analysts were engaging less critically with AI-assembled evidence, the Level 6 interface could adaptively increase the evidence engagement requirements to maintain analytical rigour.

The NRB evidence package format should be extended to support PDF/A (the ISO archival PDF standard) and digital signature using Nepal's national digital signature infrastructure, enabling NRB to verify the integrity and provenance of submitted evidence packages programmatically rather than through manual review. This extension would reduce the administrative burden of NRB compliance for both submitting organisations and NRB examination staff.

## 20 · Conclusion

This thesis has proposed, designed, developed, and empirically validated SENTINEL — Security ENforcement Through INtelligent EvaLuation — as an intelligent multi-agent automated Android application security assessment framework that addresses the critical deficit in mobile security assessment capacity documented in Nepal's Kathmandu Valley fintech ecosystem. The framework was designed from the outset as an interdisciplinary artefact, drawing simultaneously on computer science (distributed systems, machine learning, natural language processing), behavioural psychology (Prospect Theory, cognitive bias mitigation), and applied cybersecurity (OWASP MASVS taxonomy, Frida dynamic instrumentation, NRB regulatory compliance). This interdisciplinary foundation is not incidental to SENTINEL's performance but constitutive of it: the specific design choices that produce SENTINEL's superior evaluation results are each directly motivated by theoretical insights from the relevant literature.

The research confirmed both research hypotheses with substantial empirical evidence. H1 — that multi-specialised agent orchestration combining static decompilation, dynamic Frida instrumentation, RAG-enriched knowledge retrieval, and LLM-assisted verdict classification would achieve vulnerability detection coverage exceeding 80% and a false positive rate below 15% — was confirmed with results of 94.3% coverage and 8.3% false positive rate, exceeding the specified thresholds by 14.3 and 6.7 percentage points respectively. The improvements over all three baseline comparators — MobSF (52.7% coverage, 38.1% FPR), QARK (41.2% coverage, 42.4% FPR), and manual expert testing (53.4% coverage, 31.2% FPR, 7.4 hours) — are consistent, substantial, and attributable to specific architectural components of SENTINEL rather than to evaluation artefacts.

H2 — that multi-specialised agent automation provides genuine benefits that are realised safely only when a structured governance model incorporating authorisation gates, privacy-preserving analysis, algorithmic transparency, and mandatory human approval is embedded as an architectural constraint rather than a discretionary policy — was confirmed by the ethical governance audit. The authorisation gate's 100% rejection of unauthorised submissions, the successful privacy isolation across all tested cross-assessment boundary attempts, the NRB-confirmed sufficiency of the algorithmic transparency record, and the analyst engagement data from the Level 6 approval process together demonstrate that SENTINEL's governance architecture functions as designed and provides the mitigation of the principal ethical risks of AI-assisted security assessment.

The interdisciplinary synthesis that SENTINEL embodies represents one of the thesis's most significant conceptual contributions. Kahneman's (2011) Prospect Theory, applied as a design rationale for the seven-level proof gate, connects directly to the measurable false positive rate reduction achieved by that gate: the progressive evidence accumulation mechanism directly compensates for the loss aversion distortion that causes human analysts to under-investigate ambiguous alerts. The connection between behavioural economics theory and engineering design outcomes — mediated by specific architectural choices — demonstrates that interdisciplinary research in cybersecurity can produce not merely richer contextualisation but measurably superior tools.

SENTINEL is released under the MIT licence as a contribution to the Nepalese cybersecurity community and to the global open-source security research ecosystem. The NRB-compliant evidence packaging, the Nepali fintech-relevant evaluation corpus, and the honest documentation of the Nepali-script bias limitation are designed to make SENTINEL immediately useful and honestly presented to Nepalese practitioners and researchers. The framework is not presented as a replacement for human expert security assessment but as a capability multiplier that extends the reach of the skilled practitioners who are available in Nepal's constrained talent market.

The significance of SENTINEL for Nepal's fintech sector extends beyond the technical performance metrics. By providing a reproducible, systematic, and NRB-compliant assessment methodology that is accessible to organisations without the budget for commercial security platforms or the ability to retain international consultants, SENTINEL contributes to the levelling of the security capability landscape across Nepalese fintech firms of different sizes. The aspiration of the research is that the framework contributes, in some measurable way, to improving the security of the applications on which millions of Nepalese citizens increasingly depend for financial access, economic participation, and daily life.
